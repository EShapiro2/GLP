How it works

The apps on this site are run by the people who use them, on their own phones. This page explains how they differ from the apps you know, and how each is made from a few rules in English.

1Who runs an app?

Most apps run on a company's servers: the company keeps the data and sets the rules. Blockchains such as Ethereum replace the company with a global network of computers, the validators, which must all agree on one shared ledger and are paid a fee for every act. A grassroots app has neither. Each person's phone runs its own copy and keeps its own data, and an act involves only the phones of the people taking part in it.

2Start small, join later

A grassroots app needs no headquarters and no permission to start. A family, a club or a village can start using one on its own; elsewhere, another group does the same. When people from the two groups meet and agree, the two groups become one, with nothing to move or migrate. Repeated, this is how a grassroots app spreads: group by group, from the bottom up.

The idea is old. In 1863, Proudhon described federation as a pact between families, towns and groups in which each party keeps more than it gives up. Grassroots is the same idea, made precise for apps.

3What a contract is

Contracts are written in a small, precise kind of English. Each rule says who may do what, and whose agreement it needs; there is nothing else in a contract. Yet these few kinds of rules are enough for every contract on this site: social networks and group chats, personal coins and bonds, barter, short-stay rentals, a lending library, joint accounts, and DAOs with voting and liquid democracy. Here is a complete one, for a friendship app. It has two rules, on top of a basic contract about having a key and getting to know people.

◆
FriendshipAlice and Bob, who know each other personally and are not friends, may jointly decide to become friends.
■
Ending a friendshipAlice may end their friendship with Bob.

The two rules differ in whose agreement they need. Becoming friends needs both Alice and Bob. Ending a friendship takes only one of them: whoever ends it, the other is not asked. Try it:

An app can check some rules and not others: it cannot know whether Alice and Bob really know each other personally. So every rule is one of three kinds:

■

Enforced

The app makes it so.

Ending a friendship always works.
◆

Attested

You sign that it is true.

"We know each other personally."
○

Undertaken

A promise the app cannot check.

Standing behind everything signed with your key.

As with any contract, it is best made with people you trust to keep their word.

4How a contract becomes an app

This is not vibe coding. AI helps at exactly one step: translating the English rules into speech acts, a precise form that a person can read and check. Every step after that is a fixed program: the same input always gives the same output, with no guessing.

Speech acts are simple enough that a program can check, from their form alone, that the app will be grassroots, and a theorem proves that this check is enough. So every app here is grassroots by construction.

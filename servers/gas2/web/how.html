How it works

The apps on this site are run by the people who use them, on their own phones. This page explains how they differ from the apps you know, how each is made from a few rules in English, and how GAS2 puts them all in one app. The </> button on top shows the technical part.

1Who runs an app?

Most apps run on a company's servers: the company keeps the data and sets the rules. Blockchains such as Ethereum replace the company with a global network of computers, the validators, which must all agree on one shared ledger and are paid a fee for every act. A grassroots app has neither. Each person's phone runs its own copy and keeps its own data, and an act involves only the phones of the people taking part in it.

2Start small, join later

A grassroots app needs no headquarters and no permission to start. A family, a club or a village can start using one on its own; elsewhere, another group does the same. When people from the two groups meet and agree, the two groups become one, with nothing to move or migrate. Repeated, this is how a grassroots app spreads: group by group, from the bottom up.

The idea is old. In 1863, Proudhon described federation as a pact between families, towns and groups in which each party keeps more than it gives up. Grassroots is the same idea, made precise for apps.

3What a contract is

Contracts are written in a small, precise kind of English. Each rule says who may do what, and whose agreement it needs; there is nothing else in a contract. Yet these few kinds of rules are enough for every contract on this site, from social graphs and social networks to personal coins, bonds and barter. Here is a complete one, for a friendship app. It has two rules, on top of a basic contract about having a key and getting to know people.

◆
FriendshipAlice and Bob, who know each other personally and are not friends, may jointly decide to become friends.
■
Ending a friendshipAlice may end their friendship with Bob.

The two rules differ in whose agreement they need. Becoming friends needs both Alice and Bob. Ending a friendship takes only one of them: whoever ends it, the other is not asked. Try it:

An app can check some rules and not others: it cannot know whether Alice and Bob really know each other personally. So every rule is one of three kinds:

■

Enforced

The app makes it so.

Ending a friendship always works.
◆

Attested

You sign that it is true.

"We know each other personally."
○

Undertaken

A promise the app cannot check.

Standing behind everything signed with your key.

As with any contract, it is best made with people you trust to keep their word.

4How a contract becomes an app

This is not vibe coding. People write two things: the rules, and the rules in exact form, as act schemas, which a person can read and check (an AI may help write them). Every step after that is a fixed program: the same input always gives the same output, with no guessing.

Act schemas are simple enough that a program can check, from their form alone, that the app will be grassroots, and a theorem proves that this check is enough.     5One app for all of them

    You install one app, the Grassroots Super-App, and keep in it your key and your friends. Each contract is a mini-app you add to it. Your phone checks the mini-app's certificate and certifies it again under your own key, so it runs only what you added yourself.

    To use a mini-app with a friend, one of you invites the other, and the other accepts. The two phones then check that both run the very same mini-app, and from then on its acts go between the two of you only. Nearby, the phones talk over Bluetooth or the local network; farther away, through a relay, which passes messages it cannot read.

    6Try it

    Get the app for Android, Windows, macOS or Linux, or use it on the web with nothing to install (on the web, talmonlab's server runs your super-app and keeps your key). Add a friend, add a mini-app, and play.

These are the entry miniapp/4, which is the same file in every app, the platform's types written again, and a scripted person. GAS2 writes them out for each contract, using GLP's own files with only the contract's name and a short script put in. No contract needs code written by hand to become a mini-app. Where one does not get there, the contract's page says which step stopped it and why, in the tool's own words.

    A run on two super-apps

    Each run starts two smartphones, Alice's and Bob's, as two isolates of GLP's runtime. Each runs the super-app, the program in programs/social/graph/core, under its own key pair. Each person's acts on their super-app are scripted, and so are their acts in the mini-app:

    1. Each person trusts the compiler's key. Each installs the mini-app, and the super-app installs it only if its certificate is from a trusted compiler and its creator certified it as grassroots.
    2. Alice asks to be Bob's friend, and Bob says yes. They are now friends in the social graph, and each holds the attestation of their friendship, signed under their own key.
    3. Alice invites Bob to the mini-app with a signed invitation, and Bob, who said yes in advance, joins.
    4. At the handshake each side signs an undertaking to keep the contract. They compare the mini-app's source identity and the type identity of its entry, and only then does the conversation open.
    5. The super-app activates the mini-app with its grants: the person's key, the person's screen, and the channel to the social graph. In the mini-app, each person establishes an identity, and Alice proposes that the two know each other, which Bob accepts.

    For the contracts whose apps are written by hand in programs/apps, the contract's page also runs that app's own play, with its longer scripts.

    On a device

    GAS2's app (get it here) runs the same super-app, programs/social/graph/core, for a real person, with GAS2's device harness in place of the super-app's own entry superapp/3. The two differ in one thing: a mini-app the person installs is shown to the person.

    In GLP the super-app holds a mini-app's person channel at the type Opaque and can only pass it on, so nothing outside the mini-app can write the person's input into it. So each of GAS2's mini-apps carries a live person of its own. The super-app runs it at the mini-app's own types, as the plays run their scripted persons. It shows the person every draw the mini-app makes, and reads the person's answers back from strings the device writes.

    Each device certifies its mini-apps under the person's own key, so the person trusts their own compiler. Friendship, the invitation, the undertakings and the handshake are the super-app's, as in the runs above. On a phone the super-app's network is GNA's layer, Bluetooth and the local network, so the people near show on the page and are added with one tap; people not near are reached through talmonlab's relay, encrypted end to end. A computer uses the relay alone.

    On the web (for iPhones, where there is no app yet) the same page and the same super-app run for the person on talmonlab's server, over the relay. The server holds the person's key, and the page says so.

    The app maker (here) takes a contract a person writes, its text and its act schemas, down the same cascade, and gives a link. A device that opens the link adds the mini-app and certifies it under its own person's key, like the ones it came with.

    How the relations are found

    GSC defines five relations between contracts. Three are about schemas: amends deletes and adds schemas, extends amends while deleting nothing, and instantiates fills in a parametric contract's parameters. Two are about runs: implements, and restricts, which allows only runs of the base. Each contract's text names what it builds on. Where the text also says how, for example "GD-S restricts GD", the map shows that.

    Otherwise GAS2 compares the two contracts' schemas, as GSC's reader reads them, with their variables renamed in order. If every schema of the base is kept unchanged, then the contract extends it. If some are deleted or replaced, then it amends it, and the contract's page says which. Whether one contract restricts another cannot be decided in general (GSC, Proposition "Restriction is undecidable"), so restriction is shown only where a contract's text states it.
